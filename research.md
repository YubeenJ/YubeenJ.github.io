My doctoral research centers on the Gulf Stream near Cape Hatteras, where a western boundary current encounters complex topography and separates from the coast. I approach these questions through observational oceanography, integrating in situ measurements and satellite remote sensing.

## Gulf Stream variability

**How does a strong western boundary current vary through time?**

I use long-term high-frequency radar observations to characterize the Gulf Stream near Cape Hatteras, focusing on changes in its path and current structure. This work includes developing and evaluating methods to extract Gulf Stream parameters from surface-current observations.

*High-frequency radar · Time-series analysis*

## Currents, winds & topography

**What drives changes in the Gulf Stream’s path?**

I investigate equatorward shift events and their atmospheric forcing, along with current–topography interactions and Gulf Stream path coherence before separation. These questions connect mesoscale variability with the physical processes shaping western boundary currents.

*Western boundary currents · Air–sea interaction*

## Ocean structure from observations

**What can surface measurements tell us about the ocean below?**

My broader interests include connecting satellite observations with subsurface ocean structure. My previous work examined surface and subsurface warming in the East/Japan Sea, reconstructed three-dimensional temperature from sea-surface measurements, and explored interactions between eddies and the Kuroshio Current.

*Satellite remote sensing · Upper-ocean structure*

## Connecting the surface and the ocean interior

High-frequency radar offers a long-term view of surface-current variability. Gliders and shipboard measurements provide information about the ocean beneath the surface, while satellite observations place local changes in a broader spatial context.

**Tools:** HF radar · Underwater gliders · Satellite altimetry · Shipboard observations · MATLAB & Python
